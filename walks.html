Walking the graph: eight papers

Drafted graph v0.4 · steps coded from the full text of the success set · success score v0 · not yet discussed

  • For each paper: where it starts, every step the authors took with the node and the mode it belongs to, where it ends, and its success profile.
  • Map. Rust: the node where the paper enters. Teal: nodes it visits. Grey: nodes it never uses.
  • Strip. The steps in order. → a step along an edge the graph draws (or a repeat of the same node). ⇢ a jump the graph reaches only through other nodes.
  • Table. One row per step: the node, the mode of inquiry the step belongs to, what the authors did, and the page. Read the table against the paper.
  • Each node’s letter is the first letter of its verb: N new capability · E explore: establish the phenomenon · O observe · I infer a latent quantity (estimate) · Q question · H hypothesize · S simulate or derive the expectations · D design the test · F find or set the conditions · C compare · V vary and replicate · X exclude · K keep the claim · P protect · R refine. The crosswalk maps each node to the modes it serves.
  • One reader (coder 1, a Claude subagent, codebook v0) coded each paper, from the text alone; two blind model coders recoded them on October 25, 2026 (see “What the walks cannot show”). The order is the order of the paper’s account, which may not be the order of the work. Full codings: private/analysis/success-set/. Slides: three of these walks.

Is the graph right? What these eight say

Test of graph v0.4 Result on the eight papers
Every move can be placed on a node Yes: no coder needed a node outside v0.4
Transitions follow edges the graph draws 24 of 83 transitions between the 91 steps (29%); the rest are jumps
The textbook route Q → H → S → D → F → O → C is walked Never
D, a discriminating test designed before the data 1 of 8 papers
H, stated rival hypotheses 5 of 8 papers
Entry at a node other than Q or H 7 of 8 papers
  • The node set holds; the edges do not. Most science moves between nodes in ways the graph draws only as long detours.
  • The most frequent jumps: V ⇢ C in 4 papers; S ⇢ C in 4 papers; C ⇢ P in 3 papers; C ⇢ K in 3 papers; X ⇢ V in 2 papers. S ⇢ C is a derivation compared with data already in hand; C ⇢ X and C ⇢ K are verdicts without a control series, which found conditions force.
  • Next tests. The same coding on the 24 matched controls and on unsuccessful papers. If ordinary and failed papers walk the same routes, the route does not explain success; if they skip different nodes, the graph has found something.
  • Candidate changes for v0.5: draw S → C and C → X, K as edges; or make the graph two-level, modes as the outer graph and the hypothesis pass as one mode’s inner loop. The room decides.

The success score (v0)

  • Four dimensions, each scored from 0 to 1 against comparable work, then combined. No measure here is settled; the score is a scaffold the group replaces piece by piece.
Dimension v0 measure Compared against Status
Impact Citation percentile All research articles in the same journal, year, and OpenAlex subfield Measured
Breadth (polymathy) Mean of two ranks: share of citers outside geoscience; share of references outside geoscience The three matched controls Measured, coarse
Novelty Rank of CD₅ disruption The three matched controls Measured, but CD₅ is a poor proxy; to be replaced by the group’s rating on the Novelty Dossier
Advance The group’s blinded rating on the advance profile, rescaled to 0–1 Not yet rated Pending
  • Macro score. The geometric mean of the measured dimensions, each floored at 0.05. A geometric mean does not let a high score on one dimension buy back a near-zero on another, which is what “success on all four” means (OECD et al. 2008). The arithmetic mean is reported beside it as a sensitivity check.
  • What the v0 scores can and cannot say. The recognized four score high because they were chosen on citations. The designated four have 1 to 25 citers each, so their breadth and novelty ranks are noise. The weak novelty proxy sinks Gräff and Xu.

What the walks cannot show

  • Loops are under-counted by design. A published paper reports the work in the order of its argument: between protocol and paper, 62% of trials changed, added, or dropped a primary outcome (Chan et al. 2004). Our coding followed the paper’s order and had no field for a failed attempt, a rerun, or a redesign, so a return and a next step look alike.
  • Loops are at the mode level, for coder 1. Merging consecutive repeats, the papers return to a node already visited 11 times but to a mode already used 27 times; every paper has at least one return. The decision tree draws the graph at that level.
  • The blind coders do not confirm it. Two blind model coders (Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, paper codebook 1.3.0, October 25, 2026) code 169 and 108 steps on the same eight papers. They find 83 and 33 node returns against 78 and 38 mode returns, so where the loops sit depends on the coder (private/analysis/reliability/codings/papers/, final-paper sequences, consecutive repeats merged).
  • One coder per walk, coding against the graph it tests. The coder was given the v0.4 nodes, so every step was placed on a node; “no new node needed” is partly an effect of the instruction. The blind coders also get the graph. They agree on which nodes a paper uses at Krippendorff’s alpha 0.63, below the 0.67 gate, over 13 papers (private/analysis/reliability/RESULTS.md). No person has coded yet.
  • Granularity varies. Vine and Matthews have 17 steps, Normandeau and colleagues and Gräff and colleagues 8. Step counts measure the coder as much as the paper.
  • Labels are not fully consistent. One Shapiro step is labelled replication where the others use robustness.
  • Supplements unread for Shapiro, Lam, Sun, and Xu, where methods and robustness checks usually sit.
  • Successes only, as walks. The eight walks are successes. Five matched controls have been coded since (October 21 and 25, 2026); no failure has been coded.
  • How to see the loops. Peer-review files, open discussions, and version histories record changes made during the work. On nine papers with such records, 126 loops were found, and 4 of them (3%) can be read as loops in the final paper (decision tree, section 6). For Gräff and colleagues and for Lam and colleagues, a review sequence can now be coded beside the final-paper sequence.

For students

  • Pick a paper you know. Write its start, its steps as a strip, and its end. For each step, name the node, the mode, and the page.
  • Mark the node where the paper entered and the nodes it never needed. Then ask what would have made it a stronger paper, and at which node.
