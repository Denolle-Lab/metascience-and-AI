Is there a scientific method?

Meeting 1 of 13

Central question

Is hypothesis-testing a universal account of inquiry, a standard for justification, or one useful mode among several?

Why these papers belong together

Platt offers a prescriptive strategy; Cleland analyzes differences between experimental interventions and reconstructing past events. Neither paper directly records this group’s daily research. Test both against an actual project rather than declaring one a complete scientific method. (Platt 1964; Cleland 2001)

Inference, deduction, and hypothesis generation

Deduction is not the opposite of inference. It is one kind of inference. Platt’s workflow embeds deductive reasoning inside a broader process of inductive inference.

Term Definition Role in strong inference
Inference Any reasoned movement from premises or evidence to a conclusion. The general category containing deduction, induction, and other forms of reasoning.
Deduction Reasoning in which the conclusion must follow if the premises are true. Derives a predicted observation from each hypothesis: if \(H_i\) and its auxiliary assumptions hold, then \(P_i\) should occur.
Inductive inference Reasoning from observations to a broader but provisional conclusion. New evidence can revise it. Uses the observed outcome to exclude, retain, or refine hypotheses. This is what Platt calls the branching logical tree (Platt 1964, 347).
Hypothesis generation The creative proposal of possible explanations, sometimes called abduction. Produces the alternatives at the beginning of each cycle. Platt says this step requires “intellectual inventions, which must be cleverly chosen” rather than a mechanical rule, and sends the reader to Pólya for how to make them (Platt 1964, 347).

The logic within one branch. For a candidate hypothesis \(H_i\), together with auxiliary assumptions \(A\):

\[H_i + A \Rightarrow P_i\]

This movement from hypothesis to prediction is deduction.

If the experiment instead produces \(\neg P_i\), then:

\[\neg P_i \Rightarrow \neg(H_i + A)\]

This is the basis of exclusion. Strictly speaking, the test has contradicted the combination of the hypothesis and its assumptions, not the hypothesis alone. Platt does not draw that distinction; Cleland makes it the center of her case against falsificationism, with the astronomers who kept Newton and rejected an auxiliary assumption to find Neptune as the example (Cleland 2001, 988).

The resulting revision of the hypothesis set is inductive inference:

\[\{H_1, H_2, H_3\} + \text{observed outcome} \Rightarrow \text{a smaller, provisionally preferred set}\]

Observing \(P_i\) does not deductively prove \(H_i\), because another hypothesis might also predict \(P_i\). Strong inference addresses this problem by seeking observations for which the competing hypotheses make different predictions.

Platt’s branching discipline: strong inference

Platt’s strong inference is a repeating process of generating alternatives, designing an observation that distinguishes them, excluding incompatible alternatives, and refining what remains (Platt 1964, 347).

Exclusion means that an observed outcome is incompatible with a prediction required by one or more hypotheses. Those hypotheses are removed from the current candidate set. The hypothesis that remains has survived this test; it has not been proved true.

The ideal experiment is therefore not merely one that produces information. It is one for which the possible outcomes change which explanations remain viable.

Is it Baconian? Platt says so himself. He traces the “conditional inductive tree” to Bacon’s Novum Organum, with its “Instances of the Fingerpost” that mark where roads part and its rule that induction must proceed by “proper rejections and exclusions” (Platt 1964, 349), and he presents Chamberlin’s multiple working hypotheses as the second invention “needed to round out the Baconian scheme” (Platt 1964, 350; Chamberlin 1965). In today’s vocabulary the exclusion step is eliminative induction, and Platt quotes Popper for the claim that science advances only by disproofs (Platt 1964, 350). Two things in the diagram are not Baconian and not in Platt. The distinction between \(H_i\) and \(A\) above the exclusion arrow is Cleland’s (Cleland 2001, 988). And the box that generates \(H_1\), \(H_2\), \(H_3\) has no rule inside it; Platt concedes as much, and the seminar’s question is what happens there.

Compare that trajectory with the published narrative, and with Platt’s loop in the reading notes: where the project left the loop, or never entered it, is the row it contributes to the inquiry-mode ledger. Optional extensions

  • Chamberlin (1965): The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses. Science (1965). Written by a geologist, and the source Platt built on. Chamberlin’s target is the single ruling hypothesis; his remedy is to hold a family of them at once. Decide whether Platt’s loop and Chamberlin’s family are the same practice.
  • Simon (1973): Does Scientific Discovery Have a Logic?. Philosophy of Science (1973). Simon argues, against Popper, that discovery is problem solving with describable heuristics. The computer discovery programs listed in meeting 13 were built on this argument.
  • Klahr and Dunbar (1988): Dual Space Search During Scientific Reasoning. Cognitive Science (1988). People solving a discovery task searched two spaces, one of hypotheses and one of experiments, and some made progress by running experiments with no hypothesis at all. Experimental evidence that inquiry has more than one search mode.
  • Holmes (1987): Scientific Writing and Scientific Discovery. Isis (1987). Holmes compared laboratory notebooks with the published papers written from them and describes what the paper leaves out. The basis for standing question 5.

On geological method in particular. The pair above is about science in general; these are about how geologists reason, and they are the start of the answer to whether the seminar has yet reached a general geoscience workflow.

  • Gilbert (1896): The Origin of Hypotheses, Illustrated by the Discussion of a Topographic Problem. Science (1896). Grove Karl Gilbert on where a geological hypothesis comes from, worked through his own attempt to explain the Coon Butte crater: by analogy, by enumeration of the possible causes, and by the test that separates them. The geological ancestor of Platt’s method, and an account of the generative step Platt hands to Pólya.
  • Johnson (1933): Role of Analysis in Scientific Investigation. Geological Society of America Bulletin (1933). A geologist’s account of analysis as the method of geological investigation, with the failure modes it guards against.
  • Frodeman (1995): Geological reasoning: Geology as an interpretive and historical science. Geological Society of America Bulletin (1995). Geology as an interpretive and historical science, against the physics model of method. The geological statement of Cleland’s thesis, seven years earlier.
  • Kleinhans et al. (2005): Terra Incognita: Explanation and Reduction in Earth Science. International Studies in the Philosophy of Science (2005). What explanation and reduction mean in earth science, where the systems are open and the histories unique.
  • Elliott and Brook (2007): Revisiting Chamberlin: Multiple Working Hypotheses for the 21st Century. BioScience (2007). Chamberlin’s multiple working hypotheses reconsidered for present-day practice, with the model-selection tools that now implement them.
