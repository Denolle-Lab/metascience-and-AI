Glossary: modes of inquiry and terms of assessment

These are the working definitions the seminar uses. The modes of inquiry are the rows of the inquiry-mode ledger and the codebook for the corpus study. The terms of assessment are the variables meetings 10 to 12 measure. Meeting 1 revises this page, and the revised page is the seminar’s first output, the inquiry-action vocabulary. Every definition is tied to the readings that motivate it, and every mode has three coding aids: how to recognize it in a paper, what evidence it produces, and how it characteristically fails.

Every citation links to its full record in the bibliography. Nothing here is settled. A definition that fails on a paper someone in the group has written is a definition to fix, and the page records the fix.

How to use a mode

A mode of inquiry is characterized by its epistemic function, what the work does for knowledge, and by its decisive move, the action that changes what comes next. Papers mix modes; a single study can describe, then explore, then form a hypothesis, then test it (Cleland 2002). For the ledger and the corpus study, code the decisive mode and record secondary modes separately. Code the mode the paper presents; where the trajectory is documented and differs from the presentation, record both, because the gap between them is what standing question 5 is about (Holmes 1987).

Three of the rows, instruments, methods, and replication, are also contribution roles in the Scientific Advance Profile. They are modes here because each has its own epistemic function: producing data that did not exist, extracting information from data that did, and testing what survives. Instruments and methods are kept apart by that line, new data against new information from existing data, and not by whether hardware is involved. Infrastructure is not a row. A seismic network, a catalog, a software package, and a standard are infrastructure for different modes, so the ledger and the corpus study record “shared infrastructure” as a flag on any row. An automated system (King et al. 2009; Boiko et al. 2023) is not a mode of its own either: it is a method that performs some mode, and the coding question is which.

Sub-modes

The readings distinguish finer kinds of inquiry within several rows. Record the sub-mode where it is clear; it is what the ledger’s “decisive move” column will often name.

  • Under exploratory discovery: exploratory experimentation, the systematic variation of conditions without a stable hypothesis (Steinle 1997); exploratory data analysis, looking at data to find structure and questions (Tukey 1962); and serendipity, an unexpected observation recognized as worth following (Yaqub 2018).
  • Under hypothesis testing: the controlled experiment, in which the investigator produces the test condition (Cleland 2002); the discriminating observation, a test on data the system does not allow one to manipulate (Sykes 1967); and the smoking gun, a trace that picks one hypothesis out of a stated set (Cleland 2001).
  • Under theory and mechanistic modeling: conceptual synthesis, a new object that reorganizes old observations (Wilson 1965); and theory-led prediction, a derived quantity then sought (Vine and Matthews 1963).
  • Under instruments and observing systems: instrument-led observation, where the questions follow the sensor (Lindsey et al. 2019).
  • Under methods and computation: methods that create a new observable from data everyone already had, as ambient-noise interferometry did with seismic noise (Shapiro and Campillo 2004; Shapiro et al. 2005); and automated discovery, a system that performs generation, selection, or execution (Langley 1981; Kulkarni and Simon 1988), which is coded here even when it drives a laboratory, because what it adds is the computation.
  • Under synthesis and reconstruction: trace inference, explaining present traces by past events that cannot be rerun (Cleland 2002); and literature-based synthesis, new claims from connecting published results (Swanson 1986).

Terms of assessment

Meeting 1 fixes a first working definition for each of these, so that meetings 10 to 12 and the corpus study measure variables defined before the data are seen rather than after. Each entry gives the working definition, the measures the literature uses, and what those measures do not capture.

Novelty. Newness of a specific claim relative to a stated corpus and time. It is not correctness and not importance (Uzzi et al. 2013; Fontana et al. 2020). Measures: atypical combinations of cited references (Uzzi et al. 2013), distance of a paper’s words from the prior literature (Shibayama et al. 2021), and expert judgment, which disagrees with itself (Boudreau et al. 2016). Not captured: whether the claim is true, and what the searched corpus missed. The seminar’s rule is “no precedent located within this search,” never “never done before.” See meeting 10.

Depth. Concentration of a researcher’s or a paper’s competence within one area, the opposite of breadth. Measures: the concentration of an author’s publications across topics (Teodoridis 2018; Teodoridis et al. 2019; Rassenfosse et al. 2022), and for a paper, the concentration of its references in one field, which is the low end of the interdisciplinarity measure below. Not captured: depth is relative to how finely a field is divided, and the same researcher is deep by one taxonomy and broad by another.

Interdisciplinarity. Integration of knowledge from distinct fields in one piece of work, as opposed to work that merely draws on several (Wagner et al. 2011). Measures: the diversity of the fields a paper cites, counting how many fields, how evenly, and how distant they are from one another (Stirling 2007; Porter and Rafols 2009); the disciplines of the authors; and atypical reference pairs (Uzzi et al. 2013). Not captured: field taxonomies are conventions, and a diverse reference list shows what was read, not what was understood. Where such work lands is a measured question, taken up below and in meeting 11.

Impact. Downstream uptake or consequence of a contribution (Dellsén 2016). Measures: citations normalized by field and year, which makes counts comparable across fields (Radicchi et al. 2008; Waltman 2016); models of long-term citation from early history (Wang et al. 2013); and uptake that citations miss, such as reuse of data, software, and standards, and use in decisions, which is the whole of the use-inspired row above. Not captured: citations measure attention and convention as much as use (Bornmann and Daniel 2008), and they inflate over time (Petersen et al. 2025). Novelty is penalized at first and rewarded late (Wang et al. 2017).

Field-shaping. A contribution after which a field’s questions, methods, or reference set are different. Unlike novelty, it is retrospective: it is a fact about what the field did next. Measures: the disruption index, which asks whether later papers cite a work instead of its predecessors or together with them (Funk and Owen-Smith 2017; Wu et al. 2019), with the corrections its critics require (Petersen et al. 2025; Park et al. 2023); becoming canonical in a field that keeps citing it (Chu and Evans 2021); delayed recognition, the sleeping beauty (Ke et al. 2015); and the appearance of a new topic whose papers cite it. Not captured: each measure operationalizes something different, and the seminar reports a profile rather than a score. See meeting 12, whose working definitions of advance, innovation, and impact this entry defers to.

Hypothesis gate. A rule that the next action in an inquiry must be a test of a stated hypothesis. The ledger asks, for each reconstructed episode, whether such a rule would have helped, hurt, or not applied at the decisive move. It is the design choice the seminar is questioning, and its best defenders are in the reading (Platt 1964; Ioannidis 2005; King et al. 2009).

Decisive move. The action in an episode that changed what came next: a record, a pattern, a test, a concept, a computation, a capability, a convergence, a replication, or a question posed by a user. It is what the ledger codes, and it is inferred from the record, so its evidence basis is recorded beside it.

Trajectory and narrative. The trajectory is what was done, in order. The narrative is the published account. They differ (Holmes 1987), and the difference is not misconduct; but only the trajectory is evidence about how inquiry proceeds, and most of what the seminar reads is narrative. Every ledger row says which it rests on.

Where interdisciplinary work lands

The measured answer so far, which the seminar tests against its own cases. Papers that combine an unusual pairing of references with a conventional core are cited more than either alone (Uzzi et al. 2013). Researchers who work across fields publish less and are cited more (Leahey et al. 2017). Generalists outperform specialists where a field is moving fast and underperform them where it is slow (Teodoridis et al. 2019). Specialization is rewarded persistently in at least one large field (Rassenfosse et al. 2022). And the tools that lower the cost of breadth have so far narrowed what fields ask, while widening what individuals produce (Hao et al. 2026; Doshi and Hauser 2024). None of this observed this group, and the corpus study is where the group would find out whether it holds in its own literature.
